Ice jams are a major cause of flooding in Canada. They can form during freeze-up in the fall and during spring breakup, but the jams that form at breakup are usually the ones with the greater potential to cause serious flooding.

During spring breakup, or during a winter thaw, an ice jam forms when ice from the breaking upstream ice cover is carried downstream and piles up, collecting into a blockage that holds back the river.

An ice jam raises water levels for two main reasons. The jam itself can be several metres thick, and the extra ice with its very rough underside slows the flow of the river, so water backs up behind the jam and rises.

A sudden release of a major ice jam can send a surge of water rushing downstream. This surging flow can be damaging in its own right, and it is closely related to ice jam flooding.

Heavy ice formation on rivers affects flooding, particularly on rivers that flow north. Climate controls how strong the winter ice cover becomes and how it breaks up in spring, and the manner of the breakup governs how severe the jamming is.
